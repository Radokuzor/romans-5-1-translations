One verse, fifteen voices

Justified by faith, and peace with God

Therefore, since we have been justified through faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ.

This is the hinge of Paul's letter to the Romans: the argument of chapters three and four closes here, and everything after it rests on this. Fifteen English translations of the same verse, laid out plainly — so the shared spine of the sentence stands out, and the different ways of saying justified can be compared in one place.

The fifteen

Set from the oldest formal English right through to the modern versions, in the order they are usually grouped: formal and literal renderings first, then the modern ones, then the plain-English ones. Behind the English

Why the versions say it differently

Almost every difference in the fifteen readings above sits on one Greek word and one Greek verb ending, not on any disagreement about what the verse means.

The word behind "justified"

δικαιωθέντες — dikaiōthentes

From dikaioō: to declare righteous, to acquit, to pronounce not guilty. It is courtroom language — a verdict handed down — not a description of a process of becoming good. The noun form is dikaiōsis, justification; the root dikaios means just or righteous.

Because English has no single verb that carries "declared righteous" the way the Greek does, the translators split: some keep the old legal word justified, others translate the meaning out plainly as declared righteous, made right, or made acceptable.

The grammar that carries it

A participle ("having been justified") followed by a present-tense main verb ("we have peace") — the declaring comes first, and the peace follows as its result, not the other way round. Note also the tense: having been justified, a finished act. The peace with God is not something the believer is still working up to; it stands on something already done.

The same word in the same construction carries Paul's whole argument through Romans and into Galatians — the declaration is God's, and faith is what receives it.

One textual variant

"We have peace" and "let us have peace"

There is one real difference among the manuscripts, and it shows up in the translations themselves rather than in a footnote.

The Greek runs either echomen — "we have peace" — or echōmen — "let us have peace". The two differ by a single letter, and the manuscript evidence is genuinely divided, with weight on both sides.

Most modern versions print "we have peace", reading the sentence as a statement of what is already true of the believer. A number of others print "let us have peace", reading it as an appeal to live in what has been given. Among the fifteen above, the Amplified Bible's bracket [let us grasp the fact that] is the same instinct handled in the margin rather than in the text.

Either way the doctrine does not move: justification is God's declaration, received by faith, and peace with God is what follows it.

How to read the list

The four families at a glance

Formal

Follows the Greek word order and keeps the traditional vocabulary. KJV, ASV, NKJV, NASB, NRSV, ESV. The KJV and ASV drop the opening "therefore, since" into "being therefore" — older English style, same sense.

Literal

Young's Literal Translation goes closest to the Greek shape: "peace toward God" for the preposition, and "declared righteous" rather than "justified".

Modern

NIV, CSB, NET, WEB. Same content in current English; the only visible break is whether the word justified is kept or translated out as declared righteous.

Plain English

NLT, GNT, CEV. These unpack the legal term completely — "made right", "put right", "made acceptable" — and the NLT also spells out the middle clause as "because of what Jesus Christ our Lord has done for us".

What never changes

Every one of the fifteen has the same three movements: faith, being made right with God, peace with God through Jesus Christ. Nothing in the wording moves the order.

Where to look first

Read the KJV and the NLT next to each other, then the AMP. Between those three you get the older legal phrasing, the plain modern sense, and the meaning of the Greek term laid out in full.
